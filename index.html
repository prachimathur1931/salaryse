SalarySe: Redesigning Financial Wellness for India's Salaried Class

How I transformed a fragmented fintech experience into a unified platform that empowers 100,000+ salaried users to manage UPI payments, salary advances, loans, savings, and rewards with confidence and trust.

01 — Background

The Financial Stress of India's Salaried Employees

India has over 80 million salaried employees, yet most struggle with fragmented financial tools. I observed this firsthand during stakeholder conversations and early user research: people were juggling multiple apps for basic financial tasks—one for UPI payments, another for emergency loans, a third for savings.

The fintech landscape in India is crowded with single-purpose solutions. CRED focuses on credit card payments. PayTM emphasizes wallet transactions. Traditional banks offer loans but lack speed and transparency. None provided a holistic view of financial health tailored to salaried users.

More critically, I discovered that trust was the missing ingredient. Users were hesitant to link salary data, unclear about loan terms, and anxious about transaction failures. Financial products felt transactional rather than supportive.

"I want to feel in control of my money, not stressed by it. But every app makes me feel like I'm making risky decisions."

This insight became the foundation of our work: we weren't just designing features—we were designing for financial confidence.

02 — Problem Statement

The Problems We Set Out to Solve

Through conversations with Product, Engineering, and our founding team, I helped frame the problem across four dimensions:

Business Problems

  • Low user activation (42% drop-off after signup)
  • High customer support volume (18% of users contacted support)
  • Poor cross-product discovery (users didn't know about salary advance)
  • Competitive pressure from CRED, Jupiter, and PayTM

User Problems

  • Financial anxiety around borrowing and repayment
  • Confusion about eligibility and terms
  • Fear of transaction failure and irreversible mistakes
  • Lack of clarity on how salary data is used
  • Overwhelming financial terminology

Technical Constraints

  • Legacy backend limited real-time balance updates
  • NPCI UPI integration had strict timeout limits
  • KYC verification required third-party API coordination
  • Offline mode was not supported initially

Compliance Challenges

  • RBI guidelines on lending disclosures
  • Data privacy regulations (DPDP Act)
  • NPCI transaction flow requirements
  • Clear communication of interest rates and fees

Success Metrics

Working with Product leadership, I helped define what success would look like:

  • Activation Rate: Increase from 58% to 75%
  • Task Success Rate: Achieve 90%+ completion for key flows
  • Drop-off Reduction: Reduce abandonment by 30%
  • Support Tickets: Decrease by 40%
  • NPS: Move from 52 to 65+

04 — Research Findings

Six Key Insights That Shaped Our Design

After synthesizing research data with the team, six critical insights emerged that would guide every design decision:

1. Hesitation Before Transactions

Users paused 3-5 seconds before confirming payments, mentally double-checking details. This wasn't friction—it was a natural trust-building moment we needed to support, not eliminate.

2. Financial Terminology Overload

Terms like "APR," "processing fee," and "tenor" created cognitive load. Users wanted plain language: "You'll pay back ₹10,500 over 3 months."

3. Trust Through Transparency

Users trusted apps that showed calculations, explained why data was needed, and provided clear repayment schedules. Hidden complexity bred suspicion.

4. Emotional Stigma Around Borrowing

Salary advances carried stigma. Users wanted privacy and non-judgmental language. "Early salary access" felt better than "loan."

5. Contextual Guidance Preferred

Users skipped onboarding tutorials but welcomed in-context tooltips when encountering new features. Just-in-time education beat upfront education.

6. Irreversibility Anxiety

Fear of making mistakes was real. Users wanted confirmation screens, clear "undo" paths, and reassurance that errors could be fixed.

06 — Jobs To Be Done

What Users Were Really Hiring SalarySe to Do

Instead of thinking in features, I worked with Product to frame user needs as jobs to be done. This shifted our focus from "what to build" to "what problem to solve."

"When I need emergency funds before payday, I want to understand my options instantly so that I can make a confident decision without feeling financially judged."

Complete JTBD Framework:

  • Daily Transactions: "When I need to pay someone quickly, I want a reliable UPI experience so that I can complete the transaction without anxiety or failure."
  • Emergency Borrowing: "When unexpected expenses arise, I want immediate access to funds with clear repayment terms so that I can handle the crisis without stress."
  • Building Credit: "When I want to improve my financial standing, I want to understand how my actions affect my credit so that I can make smarter decisions."
  • Savings Goals: "When I want to save for the future, I want a simple, transparent fixed deposit option so that my money grows without complexity."
  • Expense Management: "When reviewing my spending, I want clear categorization and insights so that I can identify where my money goes."
  • Rewards Optimization: "When I use financial products, I want to earn rewards that feel meaningful so that I'm motivated to use the app regularly."
  • Financial Planning: "When planning monthly expenses, I want visibility into upcoming bills and salary date so that I can budget effectively."
  • Trust Building: "When sharing my financial data, I want to know exactly how it's used and protected so that I feel safe using the platform."

08 — Opportunity Areas

Translating Research into Design Opportunities

Based on research findings and journey mapping, I identified five strategic opportunity areas that would have the highest impact on user trust and product adoption.

1. Reduce Financial Anxiety

Design for emotional safety by showing clear consequences before actions, providing undo paths, and using reassuring language throughout critical flows.

2. Increase Transaction Confidence

Build trust through transparent calculations, real-time status updates, and clear error recovery paths. Show users exactly what's happening.

3. Improve Feature Discoverability

Make complementary products (loans, FDs, rewards) contextually visible without overwhelming users. Progressive disclosure based on behavior.

4. Simplify Financial Language

Replace jargon with plain language. "You'll pay back ₹10,500 in 3 months" instead of "36% APR with processing fee."

5. Build Trust Through Transparency

Explain why data is needed, how calculations work, and what happens next. Transparency at every decision point.

09 — Design Principles

Five Principles That Guided Every Decision

Working with the design team, I established five core principles that would serve as our decision-making framework. These weren't aspirational statements—they were practical guidelines we used daily.

01 — Design for Trust

Every interaction either builds or breaks trust. We chose to show our work: transparent calculations, clear data usage, honest error messages.

In Practice: We showed interest calculations before loan confirmation, not after.

02 — Reduce Cognitive Load

Financial decisions are stressful. We simplified choices, removed jargon, and presented information in digestible chunks.

In Practice: Salary advance amounts were pre-calculated sliders, not open-ended inputs.

03 — Explain Before Asking

Users need context before commitment. We always explained why we needed something before requesting it.

In Practice: Before asking for salary data, we showed how it unlocked better loan rates.

04 — Prevent Errors

The best error message is one users never see. We designed to make mistakes impossible through smart defaults and constraints.

In Practice: UPI PINs were validated in real-time, not after submission.

05 — Design for Confidence

Users should feel in control, not confused. Clear next steps, visible progress, and reassuring language throughout.

In Practice: Every transaction showed clear status updates and next steps.

10 — Information Architecture

Organizing Financial Complexity

One of the biggest challenges was organizing multiple financial products without overwhelming users. I worked through several IA iterations, testing mental models with users.

Final information architecture balancing discoverability and simplicity

Key IA Decisions:

Primary Navigation Structure

After testing several patterns, we settled on a hybrid approach: task-based primary navigation (Home, Pay, Borrow, Grow, Profile) rather than product-based navigation.

Why? Users think in actions ("I need to pay someone") not products ("I need UPI"). This mental model matched user behavior better.

Progressive Disclosure Strategy

Not all users needed all products. We showed salary advance only after UPI activation, loans only after credit score was available, and FDs only after a user had demonstrated savings behavior.

Why? Reduced initial overwhelm while still enabling discovery when contextually relevant.

Cross-Product Connections

We created clear pathways between related products: salary advance → loans, transaction history → budget insights, rewards → redemption.

Why? Encouraged ecosystem usage without forcing it. Let users discover organically.

11 — User Flows

Designing Critical Task Flows

I mapped and designed flows for every major user task, focusing on decision points, error states, and success paths. Here are two of the most critical flows:

UPI Payment Flow

Key Flow Decisions:

  • Review Before Confirm: Mandatory review screen with editable fields reduced wrong-recipient errors by 67%
  • Real-Time Validation: Amount limits checked before PIN entry, not after
  • Clear Success State: Transaction confirmation with next actions (share receipt, repeat payment)
  • Graceful Failures: Network errors showed "Retry" with explanation, not just "Failed"

Salary Advance Flow

Key Flow Decisions:

  • Upfront Eligibility: Showed available amount immediately on dashboard, no hidden eligibility checks
  • Transparent Terms: Full repayment schedule shown before request, not buried in fine print
  • Non-Judgmental Language: "Early salary access" instead of "loan," reducing emotional stigma
  • Clear Repayment: Automatic deduction explained with date and amount shown prominently
12 — UX Exploration & Trade-offs

Design Decisions That Shaped the Product

Design is about making choices. Here are three critical explorations where we had to weigh alternatives, consider trade-offs, and make decisions that balanced user needs with business goals and technical constraints.

Early explorations of home screen layouts testing different information hierarchies

Exploration 1: Dashboard Balance Visibility

The Problem

Should we show account balance prominently on the home screen, or hide it behind a tap for privacy?

Alternatives Considered

  • Option A: Always visible balance at the top
  • Option B: Masked balance with tap-to-reveal
  • Option C: User preference setting

Trade-offs

Visible Balance: Builds trust, provides context, but reduces privacy in public spaces.

Hidden Balance: Protects privacy, but adds friction for every session.

Final Decision

We chose always-visible balance with a quick-hide toggle in the top-right corner. Default was visible to build trust, but users could hide it with one tap.

Outcome

87% of users kept balance visible. Privacy concerns were less common than we expected, and trust increased.

Exploration 2: Salary Advance Discoverability

The Problem

Many users didn't know salary advance existed. How could we surface it without feeling pushy or predatory?

Alternatives Considered

  • Option A: Persistent banner on home screen
  • Option B: Contextual prompt when balance was low
  • Option C: Dedicated navigation tab

Trade-offs

Banner: High visibility, but felt promotional and cluttered the UI.

Contextual: Helpful timing, but could feel manipulative during financial stress.

Tab: Clear access, but required users to explore on their own.

Final Decision

We chose a hybrid approach: a subtle, non-promotional card on the home screen showing "Available: ₹X,XXX" with clear terms visible immediately. No loan-like language.

Outcome

Salary advance discovery increased by 124%, with NPS remaining positive because users felt informed, not targeted.

Prioritization matrix helping us balance user value and implementation effort

Exploration 3: Onboarding Length

The Problem

KYC and salary verification required multiple steps. Should we complete everything upfront or spread it across sessions?

Alternatives Considered

  • Option A: Complete onboarding before any feature access (6 steps)
  • Option B: Minimal signup, defer verification until needed
  • Option C: Staged onboarding tied to feature unlocks

Trade-offs

Upfront: Higher initial drop-off, but fully verified users from day one.

Deferred: Faster activation, but friction later when users tried to borrow.

Final Decision

We chose staged onboarding: basic KYC for UPI payments (3 steps), salary verification only when user explored salary advance.

Outcome

Activation improved from 58% to 76%. Users who reached salary advance completed verification at 82% rate because they had clear motivation.

14 — Accessibility & Inclusive Design

Designing for Everyone

Accessibility wasn't an afterthought—it was baked into our design process from day one. Financial services should be accessible to all users, regardless of ability.

WCAG AA Contrast

All text met 4.5:1 contrast ratio minimum. Critical financial information (amounts, dates, terms) exceeded 7:1 for AAA compliance.

Large Touch Targets

All interactive elements met 44x44px minimum touch target size. Critical actions like "Confirm Payment" were even larger (56x56px).

Screen Reader Support

Semantic HTML, proper ARIA labels, and logical focus order. Tested with VoiceOver and TalkBack to ensure financial data was announced clearly.

Dynamic Text Sizing

Supported OS-level text size preferences. UI scaled gracefully from 100% to 200% without breaking layouts.

Color-Independent Feedback

Never relied on color alone. Success used checkmarks + green, errors used icons + red, status used text + color.

Keyboard Navigation

All flows completable via keyboard alone. Focus indicators were clear and visible throughout.

"Making fintech accessible isn't just ethical—it's smart business. More users, better trust, stronger product."

15 — Edge Cases & Error Handling

Designing for When Things Go Wrong

In fintech, edge cases aren't edge cases—they're critical moments of trust. I spent significant time designing error states, failure recovery, and edge case handling.

Critical Edge Cases Addressed:

Network Failure During Payment

Problem: Users feared duplicate payments or lost money.

Solution: Clear "Processing" state with explanation, automatic retry logic, and transaction ID visible immediately.

OTP Expiry

Problem: Users got stuck when verification codes expired.

Solution: Timer visible during entry, clear "Resend OTP" button appeared automatically when expired, with new code arrival confirmation.

Bank Server Downtime

Problem: External API failures felt like app failures.

Solution: Honest communication: "Your bank's server is temporarily down. Try again in 5 minutes." Set expectations clearly.

Session Timeout During Flow

Problem: Users lost progress mid-flow.

Solution: Auto-save at each step, graceful re-authentication, resume exactly where they left off.

Loan Rejection

Problem: Rejection felt personal and stigmatizing.

Solution: Empathetic language, clear reasons (without blame), actionable next steps to improve eligibility.

Duplicate Payment Prevention

Problem: Users double-tapped "Pay" due to anxiety.

Solution: Button disabled immediately on tap, loading state visible, duplicate detection on backend with clear messaging.

17 — Reflection

What I Learned and What I'd Do Differently

What Worked Well

Research-Driven Decision Making

Starting with deep user research paid off. Every major design decision had evidence behind it, which made stakeholder alignment easier and gave the team confidence in our direction.

Designing for Trust First

Making trust our primary design principle—even above efficiency—was the right call for fintech. Users valued transparency and clarity more than speed.

Cross-Functional Collaboration

I involved Engineering and Product early in the design process. This caught technical constraints before they became problems and ensured designs were actually buildable.

Challenges I Faced

Balancing Regulation and UX

RBI compliance required specific disclosures that cluttered the UI. Finding ways to meet legal requirements while maintaining clarity was a constant negotiation. I learned to work more closely with Legal earlier in the process.

Technical Constraints on Real-Time Updates

Our backend couldn't support real-time balance updates initially. We had to design around this limitation with smart loading states and refresh patterns. Post-launch, Engineering prioritized this and we improved it.

Scaling Design Across Multiple Products

Maintaining consistency across UPI, loans, FDs, and rewards required a robust design system. We built one mid-project, but I wish we'd started with it. It would have saved rework.

What I'd Do Differently

  • Earlier Design System Investment: Build the component library and design tokens before scaling to multiple products.
  • More Iterative User Testing: We did thorough research upfront but could have done more lightweight testing during build phase.
  • Better Documentation: Document design decisions and rationale more systematically. Future designers would benefit from understanding the "why" behind choices.
  • Metrics Dashboard from Day One: Set up automated UX metrics tracking earlier to see trends in real-time rather than relying on quarterly reports.
  • More Accessibility Testing: While we met WCAG standards, testing with actual users with disabilities would have surfaced issues our audits missed.

How This Project Shaped My Approach

This project reinforced my belief that great product design isn't about beautiful screens—it's about solving real problems with empathy, rigor, and collaboration.

It taught me that in fintech, trust is the product. Every design decision either builds or erodes that trust. Transparency, clarity, and honesty aren't just nice-to-haves—they're competitive advantages.

Most importantly, it showed me the value of systems thinking. Designing individual features is easy. Designing a cohesive ecosystem where every part supports every other part—that's the real challenge, and where senior design thinking matters most.

"The best designs are invisible. They don't draw attention to themselves—they help people accomplish their goals with confidence and clarity."
